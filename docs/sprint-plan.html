Capacity, rebalanced

The lane budgets in the proposal do not fit the people. Each member has 9 hours a week for 13 weeks, so 116 hours each and no more — the team raised its commitment from 8 hours specifically to fund the generative-AI work. Splitting the 40 shared hours three ways puts the reconstruction lane at 113 hours against a 104-hour ceiling, while the capture lane finishes 11 hours short. That is a 20-hour swing, and it lands on the one lane carrying the most technical risk.

Biweekly progress reviews

The course requires a progress presentation every two weeks. These are graded deliverables, so they are planned work rather than something absorbed on the day. Assumed cadence: every second Friday from 11 September — confirm the real dates with the instructor. If they shift, the mapping below still holds, because each review shows whatever gate most recently cleared.

#DatePositionWhat we demo
R1Fri Sep 11inside S1The spike: a 3D mesh built from real phone photographs. Which reconstruction engine we chose, and why.
R2Fri Sep 25S1 boundary · M1Live, end to end: phone, upload, 3D model in the browser.
R3Fri Oct 9S2 boundary · M2Guidance arrow driving capture, the timing table, and the live-versus-batch decision.
R4Fri Oct 23inside S4Measured accuracy and the noise floor per reference tier. Alignment working on printed parts.
R5Fri Nov 6inside S5Live: a planted defect comes back as a failure with the region highlighted.
R6Fri Nov 20inside S6Feature-complete walkthrough, including the intake pre-check and the unverifiable outcome.
R7Fri Dec 4S7 boundary · M7Test results, the recorded demo, and what we would do next.

Why this cadence works

Three reviews land exactly on sprint boundaries. The other four fall about a week after a gate closes, which is better than landing on one: we demo work that has been finished and tested for a few days rather than something that started working that morning. Every review in the table has a gate cleared within the preceding week, so there is never a review with nothing to show.

The standing template

Four slides, the same every time, so preparing one is assembly rather than invention.

  1. What we said we would do last time.
  2. What works now — always something running, never only slides.
  3. What slipped, and why.
  4. What we will have by the next review.

Cost, stated honestly. Seven reviews at about 2.5 person-hours each is 18 person-hours, drawn from the reserve, which falls from 45 hours to 27. That is the real price of a recurring public commitment. It stays at 18 only if each review repackages the sprint demo using the template above; if every review becomes bespoke slide-building, the reserve is gone before November. From R2 onward, keep a screen recording of the current working path — if the network or the pod fails on the day, play it and keep talking.

04

What keeps the lanes independent

Three lanes on one pipeline are naturally coupled: the app needs a service to talk to, the service needs photographs to process, and the inspection logic needs a finished model to measure. Four artifacts break all three couplings. Each one is cheap, each is built in the first two sprints, and each replaces a teammate's unfinished work with something good enough to develop against.

Contract · built S0

The frozen interface

One openapi.yaml in the repository describes every request and response before either side is written. The capture lane codes against it, the reconstruction lane implements it. Changing it requires all three members to agree at a sprint boundary, never mid-sprint.

Frees: capture from backend, viewer from inspection
Fixture · built S0

The golden capture

Thirty photographs of one printed part on a printed board, plus that part's design file and its caliper measurements, committed once and never changed. The reconstruction and inspection lanes develop against this set all semester.

Frees: reconstruction and inspection from the phone app entirely
Stub · built S1

The synthetic mesh generator

Sixty lines that take a design file, apply a random rotation, add measurement noise at a chosen level, delete a surface patch, and label vertices seen or unseen. It produces exactly what the reconstruction lane will eventually produce, months earlier.

Frees: the entire inspection lane, from sprint one
Stub · built S1

The mock service

A forty-line stand-in that answers every call in the frozen contract with a canned model file and a canned verdict. The capture lane builds the whole capture experience, viewer and report page against it without a graphics card in the loop.

Frees: capture front end from the graphics service

07

Working agreements

Rules that keep lanes apart

  1. One public repository, three top-level directories matching the three lanes, with review rules in section 08. Two people editing the same file is the exception, so merge conflicts stay rare by structure rather than by discipline.
  2. Nobody blocks for more than one day. If a teammate's real component is not ready, use the stub and keep moving. Raise it at the next standup, not by waiting.
  3. The interface contract changes only at a sprint boundary, with all three present. Mid-sprint contract drift is what turns three independent lanes back into one tangled one.
  4. No story crosses a gate. Anything unfinished at a sprint boundary is either cut or explicitly carried, never silently rolled forward.
  5. Work in progress is capped at two stories per person. Three half-finished features at a milestone is worse than one finished one.

Cadence

  • Written standup, Monday and Thursday. Three lines each in a shared channel: done, next, blocked. No meeting.
  • Sprint boundary call, 30 minutes. Demo what works, check the gate, agree the next sprint's stories, one retro question. Held on the milestone date.
  • Biweekly progress review, every second Friday. The graded class presentation. Uses the standing four-slide template and always shows something running.
  • Integration points are calendar events, not intentions. During I1 through I4 all other work stops until the pieces talk to each other.
  • Ceremony overhead is about 15 minutes per person per day and is already inside the eight hours a week, not added on top.

Definition of done

  • Reviewed and approved by one other member in a pull request
  • Merged to the main branch
  • Runs on a second member's machine from a clean checkout
  • Has one automated test or one written manual check
  • Demonstrated at the sprint boundary call
08

Repository and code review

One public repository on GitHub. Every change reaches main through a pull request approved by at least one other member. Deployment runs only from main, so deployment is gated by construction rather than by a second approval step.

Why public

Branch protection, rulesets and deployment approval gates are all free on public repositories. On private ones, branch protection needs GitHub Pro and the deploy-time gate needs Enterprise. Public also sidesteps a trap: a team Organization on the free tier cannot protect private branches even if one member holds Pro, because Pro is a personal plan.

Consequences we accept deliberately: secret scanning and push protection are on by default and will block a committed key before it reaches the remote; nothing sensitive goes in the repository at all; and a LICENSE lands in week one. Our own code can be permissive, with a README note that the reconstruction weights are non-commercial, which constrains reuse rather than publication.

Branch protection on main

  1. Require a pull request before merging
  2. Required approvals: 1
  3. Dismiss stale approvals when new commits are pushed
  4. Require status checks to pass
  5. Require branches to be up to date before merging
  6. Block force pushes and branch deletion
  7. Do not allow bypassing the above settings — the one teams forget, and without it an administrator merges straight past the rule

Pull request rules

  • One or two work sessions per pull request. Review quality collapses on large diffs.
  • Merge within a day or two. Long-lived branches quietly destroy the lane independence this plan rests on.
  • Review within 24 hours, matching the rule that nobody blocks for more than a day. Reviewing is the first thing you do in a work session, not the last.
  • Never review formatting. The linter runs in CI and wins those arguments for us.
  • The interface contract needs two approvals, because it is the surface all three lanes build against.
  • Branch names carry the lane: capture/…, recon/…, inspect/…

CODEOWNERS

The three lanes map straight onto it, so the right reviewer is requested automatically.

/capture/     @member-a
/recon/       @member-b
/inspect/     @member-c
/contracts/   @member-a @member-b @member-c

CI on every pull request

Keep the whole run under about two minutes, or people start routing around it.

LaneChecks
Pythonruff lint and format check, mypy on changed modules, pytest on the fast unit tests
TypeScripteslint, prettier check, tsc --noEmit, vitest
Inspectionverdict and registration tests run against the synthetic mesh generator, so they need no GPU and no fixtures

The synthetic mesh generator earns its keep twice: it unblocks the inspection lane in sprint one, and it makes those tests fast and hardware-free so they run on every pull request. Reconstruction tests need a GPU and stay out of CI; they run locally before a release and at each integration point.

Never commit these

  • The golden capture set. Thirty photographs is hundreds of megabytes, and git never forgets — a large file bloats the repository permanently even after deletion. Keep fixtures in cloud storage behind a download script; commit the script and a checksum.
  • Model weights. Same reason, larger. Downloaded at setup.
  • Any key or token. Actions secrets, plus a gitignored local env file.

Cost, and the tripwire

Review time sits inside the existing lane estimates the same way writing tests does; it is part of shipping a change, not extra to it. Budget roughly half an hour per person per week. If reviews start taking materially longer than that, the pull requests are too big. That is the earliest warning signal this plan has, and the fix is smaller pull requests, not less review.

Setup is about two hours in S0: create the repository, protect main, add CODEOWNERS, and stand up a CI skeleton that runs nothing but the linters on day one.

Feeding the biweekly review: link every pull request to an issue, and the issues closed since the last class presentation become the “what we did” slide.

09

Stories

Backend first. The reconstruction engine — photographs in, metric 3D model out — is built before the other layers, so the hardest risk is retired before anything is built on top of it. The full backlog lives in proofshape_stories.md: 22 stories written out for foundations and the engine, 93 hours, with everything after that left as titles until its sprint planning.

How stories are picked up

  • Lanes become places, not people. The three directories stay; any member picks up any story in any of them. That is what lets all three push on the engine at once.
  • One person per story, claimed visibly before work starts. Stuck is fine and asking for help is expected; two people quietly working the same story is not.
  • Either of the other two reviews. No fixed reviewer per area.
  • Completion is recorded on the story: who, the date, the pull request, and actual hours against the estimate.

Story states

  1. Blocked — a dependency is unfinished. Do not start it.
  2. Ready — dependencies done, nobody has claimed it. Anyone can take it.
  3. Claimed — a name is on it, visibly, before work begins.
  4. In review — pull request open, waiting on one approval.
  5. Done — merged, with the completion record filled in.

The chain sets the date, not the hour total

Ninety-three hours across three people at 27 hours a week looks like three and a half weeks. It is not. About 42 of those hours can only run one story after another, and a serial chain advances at one person’s pace no matter how many people are free:

F-02 → F-07 → R-01 → R-04 → R-06 → R-07 → R-08 → R-14 → R-15

That is roughly five weeks. Adding a third person does not shorten it. Two consequences: the chain is always somebody’s top priority and gets handed off the same day if its owner stalls; and the September 25 milestone is tight, since a working endpoint needs about 40 hours of that chain. We leave the date as it stands and revisit at the second sprint boundary with real velocity rather than estimates.

The off-chain stories — board detection, intrinsics, the scale check, provenance, uncertainty, both fallback backbones and the latency table — are where the other two people work while the chain advances. There is enough of that to keep everyone busy, which is the point of laying the backlog out this way. Three stories have no dependencies at all and are the ones to start on day one, one each: the public repository, the GPU pod, and the golden capture set.

10

Buffer and the cut list

Sprints commit required and expected work plus the seven biweekly reviews and the generative-AI work, which is 324 of the 349 available hours. The remaining 25 hours are not scheduled. They absorb integration friction, bugs, and the sprint that runs long, and only what is left over buys optional features. This is why the plan can carry the proposal's full scope without pretending the team has more time than it does. The reviews are why that reserve is thinner than it first looks, and why the four-slide template is worth keeping fixed.

When a sprint runs short, cut in this order, and record the cut at the sprint boundary rather than deciding again each time:

Visual hole filling→ Defect-aware guidance→ Session integrity→ Live capture loop→ Report polish→ Report narrative

Three things are never cut. Batch capture survives even if the live loop goes, so a working phone-to-model pipeline always exists. The rule that unseen geometry can neither pass nor fail a part stays in force regardless of schedule, because without it the report means nothing. And the generative components, the capture agent, shape completion, vision-language capture supervision and the plain-English inspection spec, carry the same protection as the core, because they are a course requirement.

The cut list used to be upside down. Its previous ordering dropped the report narrative and the frame checks first, which were the only generative-AI components in the project. Under any schedule pressure it would have cut precisely what the course grades. Those components are now protected, and the narrative sits last among the cuttable items rather than first.
